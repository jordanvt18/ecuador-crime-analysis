Chapter 2: The Geography of Violence

Crime in Ecuador is not random — it follows geographic fault lines. Coastal provinces, border regions, and major port cities concentrate the vast majority of violence.

Hotspot Detection (Getis-Ord Gi*)

Using spatial statistics, we identified statistically significant crime clusters. The Pacific coast — Guayas, Esmeraldas, Manabí, Los Ríos, Santa Elena, and El Oro — forms a continuous high-violence corridor driven by drug trafficking routes, port access, and gang territorial competition.

Chapter 3: The Spillover Effect

Our Violence Pressure Map reveals that crime doesn't respect administrative boundaries. Neighboring cantons feel the pressure of regional conflict.

On average, cantons experience a +28% spillover effect — meaning nearby violence raises local pressure significantly above the canton's own baseline.

What This Means

  1. Geographic concentration requires targeted intervention. The Pacific corridor needs focused state presence.
  2. Socioeconomic drivers demand long-term investment. Law enforcement alone is insufficient — poverty, education, and inequality gaps fuel recruitment.
  3. Prison reform is urgent. The penitentiary system acts as a crime multiplier, not just containment.
  4. Cross-border dynamics matter. Ecuador's geography makes it a transit hub — regional cooperation is essential.
  5. Data transparency enables accountability. This project demonstrates what's possible with public data — more granular, timely data from authorities would strengthen analysis.
